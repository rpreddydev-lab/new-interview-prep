META — CORE VALUES & BEHAVIORAL INTERVIEW GUIDE
================================================================================

META'S APPROACH TO BEHAVIORAL INTERVIEWS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Meta's behavioral interviews are called "Jedi Interviews" (short for
Judgment, Ethics, Decisiveness, and Influence). They are structured around
Meta's core values and hiring philosophy.

Meta interviewers are specifically trained to probe for:
  → Scope and impact of your contributions
  → How you work with others (cross-functional, cross-team)
  → Speed of iteration and comfort with ambiguity
  → How you handle failure, setbacks, and criticism
  → Whether you take initiative or wait to be told what to do

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
META'S CORE VALUES (POST-2022 FRAMEWORK)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

1. MOVE FAST
   The original "Move Fast and Break Things" has evolved — it's now about
   moving fast WITH stability. Meta values speed of iteration, willingness
   to ship and learn, and not being paralyzed by the pursuit of perfection.

   Interview angle: "Tell me about a time you shipped quickly and iterated
   based on feedback rather than waiting for the perfect solution."

   What they want to see: You shipped something imperfect, got signal,
   and improved quickly. You don't over-engineer before validating.

2. FOCUS ON LONG-TERM IMPACT
   Meta asks "What matters most?" constantly. They want engineers who can
   separate important from urgent, and invest in things that compound.

   Interview angle: "Tell me about a time you invested in something that
   had long-term value, even when short-term options were available."

   What they want to see: You made a less obvious choice that paid off.
   You thought beyond the current sprint or quarter.

3. BUILD AWESOME THINGS
   Meta rewards craft and technical excellence. This isn't a value that
   gets assessed in behavioral interviews directly, but it shows up in
   HOW you describe what you build — with pride, specificity, and care.

4. LIVE IN THE FUTURE
   Meta invests in what the world will need, not just what exists. For
   engineers, this means curiosity about emerging tech, comfort with
   problems that don't have established solutions, and a builder mindset.

   Interview angle: "Tell me about a time you explored a technology or
   approach before it was mainstream in your organization."

5. BE OPEN
   Meta values transparency, intellectual honesty, and direct communication.
   "Being open" means sharing information, admitting what you don't know,
   and operating with transparency rather than politics.

   Interview angle: "Tell me about a time you shared bad news or delivered
   difficult feedback."

6. BUILD SOCIAL VALUE
   Originally the social mission — connecting the world. For engineers,
   this translates to: does your work have real-world impact for real users?

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT META SPECIFICALLY ASSESSES IN BEHAVIORAL ROUNDS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

META FOCUSES HEAVILY ON SCOPE AND SCALE:
  → "How many users were impacted?"
  → "What was the dollar value of the impact?"
  → "How many engineers did you influence?"
  → "What was the scope of the system?"

Meta calibrates seniority by the scope of the problem you solved and the
influence you had. A senior engineer should have stories that demonstrate
impact across teams, not just individual contributions.

SENIORITY CALIBRATION AT META:
  E3 (Junior): Owns tasks and features within a well-defined project
  E4 (Mid): Owns features and small projects; begins influencing peers
  E5 (Senior): Owns projects that affect multiple teams; drives decisions
  E6 (Staff): Owns org-level initiatives; creates leverage across teams
  E7 (Principal): Owns company-level bets; sets technical direction

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
META'S MOST COMMON BEHAVIORAL QUESTIONS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. "Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a coworker or cross-functional
    partner. How did you resolve it?"
2. "Tell me about a time you took initiative on a project that wasn't
    assigned to you."
3. "Describe a time when you had to make a decision with limited data."
4. "Tell me about a project that failed. What happened and what did
    you learn?"
5. "Describe a time you influenced a technical decision across teams."
6. "Tell me about a time you had to navigate competing priorities."
7. "Tell me about a time you gave or received critical feedback."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FULL EXAMPLE — META-CALIBRATED ANSWER
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Question: "Tell me about a time you took initiative on something important
           that wasn't assigned to you."

ANSWER (E5 calibrated):

[SITUATION]
"Last year, I was working on the notifications platform team at my company.
 During a sprint retrospective, I noticed from our analytics data that push
 notification opt-out rates had spiked to 38% in the past quarter — nearly
 double what they were a year before. Nobody on the team was actively
 tracking this metric and there was no roadmap item to address it."

[TASK]
"This wasn't in my scope. My team was responsible for notification delivery
 infrastructure, not engagement metrics. But I felt like this was a real
 user problem — people were turning off notifications because we were spamming
 them — and if left unaddressed, it would erode the entire value of the
 notifications platform I was helping to build."

[ACTION]
"I spent two days doing a self-directed analysis. I pulled data from three
 sources: notification send volume by type, user opt-out events, and user
 session data after receiving notifications. I found a clear pattern: 71%
 of opt-outs happened within 48 hours of receiving more than 12 notifications
 in a 24-hour window.

 I wrote a 2-page document with the data, the root cause hypothesis, and
 a proposed solution: a per-user notification rate limiter with a 10/day
 default, configurable per notification type. I also proposed a quick A/B
 test to validate the fix before full rollout.

 I shared the doc with my manager and the product manager for the feature
 teams that sent the most notifications. I was clear that I wasn't asking
 to own the solution — I just wanted to surface the problem with enough
 analysis that someone could act on it.

 The PM loved the analysis. She didn't have this data. We collaborated on
 a proposal that went to leadership, and I was asked to lead the engineering
 implementation since I already had the context."

[RESULT]
"We shipped the rate limiter with an A/B test in three weeks. In the
 treatment group, notification opt-out rates dropped from 38% to 19% — a
 50% reduction. Long-term notification reach (the percentage of users still
 opted in after 90 days) improved by 23 percentage points.

 The work also created a new shared service that 4 other teams adopted over
 the following quarter to apply rate limiting to their own notification types."

[REFLECTION]
"The lesson I took from this is that the best problems to work on are often
 the ones that fall between the cracks — nobody owns them because they don't
 fit neatly in any one team's scope. Once you're senior enough to recognize
 those gaps, you have a responsibility to fill them."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
META-SPECIFIC SIGNALS INTERVIEWERS LISTEN FOR
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Proactive, not reactive — you found the problem before it escalated
  ✓ Used data to build the case
  ✓ Demonstrated broad thinking beyond your immediate scope
  ✓ Collaborated rather than competed
  ✓ Had measurable impact (%, users, $)
  ✓ Reflected with learning, not just pride

  ✗ Waited to be assigned the work
  ✗ Solved only your own team's piece of the problem
  ✗ Cited no data or metrics
  ✗ Presented outcome as team achievement without your specific role

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PRACTICE QUESTIONS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. Tell me about a time you made a significant technical decision under
   time pressure.
2. Give me an example of a time you had to build alignment across multiple
   teams with conflicting priorities.
3. Tell me about a time you shipped something you knew was imperfect.
   What was the tradeoff you made?
4. Describe a time you received tough feedback. How did you respond?
5. Tell me about something you built that you're genuinely proud of and why.
6. Describe a time you were the driving force behind a cross-team initiative.
