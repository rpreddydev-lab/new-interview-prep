DEALING WITH AMBIGUITY — COMPLETE GUIDE
================================================================================

WHY AMBIGUITY IS ASSESSED
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Most important problems don't come with clear specifications. The best
engineers are the ones who can operate effectively when:

  • Requirements are incomplete or contradictory
  • The right approach is genuinely unclear
  • Success criteria haven't been defined
  • Priorities shift mid-project
  • The problem itself is poorly understood

At Amazon, "Bias for Action" directly addresses this: "Many decisions and
actions are reversible and do not need extensive research. We value calculated
risk taking."

At senior+ levels, the ability to structure and navigate ambiguity is often
what distinguishes "Strong Hire" from "Hire."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TYPES OF AMBIGUITY IN ENGINEERING WORK
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
REQUIREMENTS AMBIGUITY
  • The "what" is unclear or underspecified
  • Example: "Build a recommendation system" — no definition of algorithm,
    data sources, or success metrics

TECHNICAL AMBIGUITY
  • Multiple viable technical approaches with unclear trade-offs
  • Example: "Should we use a distributed cache or an in-process cache?"

ORGANIZATIONAL AMBIGUITY
  • Unclear ownership, decision authority, or stakeholder alignment
  • Example: "Who owns the data pipeline — Platform or Data teams?"

PRIORITY AMBIGUITY
  • Multiple important things competing for limited resources
  • Example: "We need to fix performance AND ship the new feature AND
    reduce technical debt — all this quarter."

GOAL AMBIGUITY
  • The success criteria are vague or undefined
  • Example: "Make the product faster."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT INTERVIEWERS ARE ASSESSING
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  • Do you get stuck waiting for perfect clarity, or do you create clarity?
  • Do you make structured decisions even with incomplete data?
  • Do you take reversible actions to learn and reduce uncertainty?
  • Do you communicate the ambiguity and your approach to stakeholders?
  • Do you set clear success criteria when none are given?

RED FLAGS:
  ✗ "I waited until the spec was finalized before starting."
  ✗ "I just did whatever the PM said, even though I wasn't sure."
  ✗ "I escalated immediately and waited for an answer."
  ✗ No attempt to understand the root problem before jumping to solutions

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE FRAMEWORK FOR DEALING WITH AMBIGUITY
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 1: ACKNOWLEDGE THE AMBIGUITY
  Don't pretend it doesn't exist. Name it.
  "This problem isn't well-defined yet. Here's what's unclear."

STEP 2: IDENTIFY WHAT YOU CAN DECIDE NOW vs. WHAT NEEDS RESOLUTION
  Not all ambiguity needs to be resolved before you start.
  Some decisions can be deferred; others must be made first.
  Ask: "What's the minimum I need to know to take the next step?"

STEP 3: GENERATE AND EVALUATE OPTIONS
  Create 2–3 concrete options. For each:
  → What are the assumptions?
  → What are the risks?
  → What's reversible vs. irreversible?

STEP 4: MAKE A CALL — OR PROPOSE ONE
  Don't over-analyze. Pick the best option with current information.
  Document the decision and reasoning.
  Identify the earliest trigger that would cause you to revisit it.

STEP 5: LEARN AND ADJUST
  Treat early progress as a probe. What did you learn?
  How does it update your understanding of the problem?

STEP 6: COMMUNICATE YOUR APPROACH
  Tell your stakeholders: "The requirements for X aren't fully defined.
  My plan is to [approach]. I'll revisit [decision point] in [timeframe].
  Assumptions I'm making: [list]. Risks: [list]."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FULL EXAMPLE — AMBIGUOUS REQUIREMENTS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Question: "Tell me about a time when you had to work with very unclear or
           ambiguous requirements."

ANSWER:

[SITUATION]
"In early 2024, I was asked to 'improve search performance' on our platform.
 The request came from our CPO, who had heard from three enterprise clients
 that search was 'slow and inaccurate.' That was the entire brief — no
 metrics, no target, no technical scope, and no definition of 'inaccurate.'"

[TASK]
"I was the senior engineer on the platform team. There was no PM assigned
 initially. I needed to define the problem clearly before I could solve it."

[ACTION]
"I resisted the temptation to immediately start optimizing queries. Instead,
 I spent the first week doing discovery:

   1. DEFINE THE CURRENT STATE: I instrumented our search logs to measure
      actual P50/P95/P99 latency and found median search latency was 3.2
      seconds — but 95th percentile was 11 seconds. The issue was in the
      tail, not the average.

   2. UNDERSTAND 'INACCURATE': I reviewed 100 random search sessions and
      flagged patterns where users refined their query 3+ times (signal of
      poor relevance). I also reached out to 2 of the 3 clients directly
      and learned that 'inaccurate' meant results showed archived/inactive
      items that polluted the result set — not a ranking problem.

   3. SET EXPLICIT SUCCESS CRITERIA: With this data, I wrote a 1-page
      problem statement: 'Reduce P95 search latency from 11s to < 2s, and
      reduce the proportion of search sessions with 3+ query refinements from
      18% to < 8%, within one quarter.'

   4. PRIORITIZE: I identified 3 interventions: query optimization (high
      impact, 2 weeks), result set filtering (medium impact, 1 week), and a
      full search reindex with proper item status filtering (high impact, 4
      weeks). I recommended starting with filtering + query optimization in
      parallel, and planning the reindex for the following sprint.

   5. VALIDATE MY APPROACH: I shared the problem statement with the CPO and
      the PM before starting any implementation. Getting alignment on the
      PROBLEM first prevented the classic mistake of solving the wrong thing."

[RESULT]
"After 6 weeks, P95 latency dropped from 11 seconds to 1.8 seconds. The
 proportion of high-refinement sessions dropped from 18% to 6%. Two of the
 three enterprise clients specifically mentioned search improvement in their
 quarterly business review. One expanded their contract.

 More importantly, by spending one week on discovery, I avoided spending 4+
 weeks on the wrong solution (relevance tuning, which was NOT the core issue)."

[REFLECTION]
"When given an ambiguous problem, the biggest trap is to immediately jump to
 solutions. The first investment should always be in understanding the actual
 problem — which often turns out to be different from the stated one."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MAKING DECISIONS WITH INCOMPLETE DATA
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A related sub-topic: "Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without
all the information you needed."

KEY PRINCIPLES TO DEMONSTRATE:
  • You gathered the CRITICAL information efficiently (not all information)
  • You identified what was knowable vs. unknowable in the time available
  • You made a reversible decision where possible to limit downside risk
  • You documented your assumptions and reasoning
  • You set a trigger to revisit the decision if new data arrived

EXAMPLE FRAMING:
  "I didn't have complete data on traffic volume for the new service. Rather
  than delay the decision, I made a 'start small and scale' architectural
  choice — designed for 10x current load but with a clear migration path to
  100x. This was reversible: we could swap the architecture within one sprint
  if we underestimated. I documented this assumption explicitly in the design
  doc and set a review trigger at 60% of estimated capacity."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
KEYWORDS AND PHRASES TO USE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  "I structured the ambiguity by breaking it into what we knew vs. what we
   needed to find out."
  "I did a discovery sprint before committing to an approach."
  "I made a time-boxed bet: if X wasn't true, we'd pivot in [timeframe]."
  "I identified the reversible vs. irreversible decisions and started with
   the reversible ones."
  "I set explicit success criteria before starting, because none were given."
  "I made a call with the best available data and documented my assumptions."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PRACTICE QUESTIONS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. Tell me about a time you were given a vague or ambiguous project and how
   you made progress.
2. Describe a time you had to make a significant decision without all the
   data or information you wanted.
3. Tell me about a time requirements changed significantly mid-project. How
   did you adapt?
4. Describe a time you had to define the problem yourself because it wasn't
   given to you clearly.
5. Tell me about a time you had to balance moving fast with the risk of
   being wrong.
