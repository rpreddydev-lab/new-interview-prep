================================================================================
MENTORING & COACHING — COMPLETE GUIDE
================================================================================

WHY MENTORING QUESTIONS ARE ASKED
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Companies — especially at senior and staff levels — don't just want great
individual contributors. They want people who MULTIPLY the team's output by
investing in the growth of others. This is a core differentiator between:

  • A strong IC who does great work
  • A senior person who makes everyone around them better

Amazon calls this "Hire and Develop the Best."
Google calls it "Leadership."
Meta looks for people who "build the team."

At the Staff/Principal level, the ability to develop talent is often weighted
as heavily as technical output.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT INTERVIEWERS ARE ASSESSING
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  • Do you invest in others genuinely (not just when it's convenient)?
  • Can you adapt your mentoring style to different people?
  • Do you give honest, constructive feedback — including difficult feedback?
  • Do you create opportunities for others to grow vs. hoarding challenge?
  • Did your mentees actually improve in measurable ways?
  • Are you motivated by others' success (not threatened by it)?

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MENTORING vs. MANAGING vs. COACHING
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MENTORING: Sharing experience, guidance, and wisdom from someone further
           along the path. Long-term, relationship-based, holistic.
           "Here's what I've learned that might help you."

COACHING:  Helping someone discover their own answers through questions.
           Short-term, goal-focused, performance-oriented.
           "What do you think the right approach is? What are the options?"

MANAGING:  Directing and evaluating — formal authority, accountability.

Most IC mentoring stories involve a blend of mentoring + coaching.
For behavioral interviews, focus on:
  • The individual's growth arc
  • YOUR specific actions that contributed to it
  • Concrete outcomes for the person and team

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
COMMON MENTORING QUESTION TYPES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. "Tell me about a time you mentored a junior or mid-level engineer."
2. "Describe a time you helped someone work through a performance challenge."
3. "Tell me about a time you gave feedback that was hard to deliver."
4. "Describe a time you developed someone's technical or leadership skills."
5. "Tell me about a time you sponsored someone for a stretch assignment."
6. "Describe a situation where a person on your team was struggling and how
    you helped them."
7. "What's your approach to mentoring and growing engineers?"

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
STORY FRAMEWORK — MENTORING A JUNIOR ENGINEER
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SITUATION:
  Describe who the person was (level, background, challenges), why they
  needed support, and why YOU took on that mentoring relationship.

TASK:
  What was your specific role? Did you take initiative to mentor them, or
  were you asked by a manager? What was the goal?

ACTION:
  This is the most important part. Be specific:
  → How did you assess their strengths and gaps?
  → What specific activities did you set up? (1:1s, pairing sessions, etc.)
  → How did you adapt your style to their learning needs?
  → What feedback did you give — and how did you deliver it?
  → How did you handle setbacks in their growth?
  → How did you create growth opportunities (stretch assignments, visibility)?

RESULT:
  → What did they achieve because of your support?
  → Promotion? Performance rating improvement? Successful project delivery?
  → How did their confidence or independence change?
  → How did this impact the team?

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FULL EXAMPLE — MENTORING A STRUGGLING ENGINEER
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Question: "Tell me about a time you helped someone on your team who was
           struggling."

ANSWER:

[SITUATION]
"About two years ago, a junior engineer joined our backend team straight out
 of university. He was technically strong on algorithms, but was struggling
 with large codebases, production readiness, and translating requirements into
 working code. After 6 weeks, his manager (not me) mentioned to me informally
 that he was behind on his ramp expectations."

[TASK]
"I wasn't his formal mentor — I was a senior engineer on the same team. But
 I could see that he was getting frustrated and starting to disengage. I
 decided to step in proactively. My goal was to help him build confidence and
 independent problem-solving skills, not just unblock him on tasks."

[ACTION]
"First, I asked him for a 30-minute coffee chat to understand his perspective.
 He shared that he felt overwhelmed by the codebase complexity and was afraid
 to ask too many questions. That told me his problem was partly confidence and
 partly not having the right mental models.

 I set up a standing weekly 45-minute session with him — split between:
   20 min reviewing a recent piece of code together (his or mine) and
   25 min on a 'concept of the week' relevant to what he was working on.

 I also started involving him in code reviews — not to critique him, but to
 ask his opinion first: 'What do you see here? What would you flag?' This
 built his pattern recognition and gave him a safe space to be wrong.

 When he made mistakes, I was deliberate about framing feedback as systems,
 not personal failure: 'The system didn't make it obvious that X was needed
 — here's how I would have navigated that. Let's document it so the next
 engineer doesn't hit the same wall.'

 After 8 weeks, I gave him a small feature to own end-to-end. I told him I
 was available for questions but he should try to drive it independently and
 document blockers before asking me. He delivered it with minimal support."

[RESULT]
"By month 5, he was fully ramped and delivering at or above expectations.
 His manager told me at the end-of-cycle review that he'd been flagged as
 a strong performer. He went on to onboard the next new hire six months later.

 For me personally, this experience shaped how I think about growth: the
 fastest way to build someone's independence is to create structured
 opportunities for them to be right — not to protect them from being wrong."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
GIVING DIFFICULT FEEDBACK — DEEP DIVE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
One of the most probed mentoring sub-topics is: "Tell me about a time you
gave difficult feedback."

WHAT INTERVIEWERS ASSESS:
  • Did you give the feedback at all? (Avoidance = red flag)
  • Was the feedback specific and actionable?
  • Was it delivered privately and respectfully?
  • Did you check for understanding and follow through?
  • Did the recipient's behavior or performance change?

THE FEEDBACK FORMULA (SBI MODEL):
  SITUATION — Describe the specific situation (when, where)
  BEHAVIOR  — Describe the observable behavior, not personality
  IMPACT    — Describe the impact on the team, project, or user

  EXAMPLE:
  ✗ "You're too negative in meetings."
  ✓ "In last Tuesday's sprint review [situation], when you said the
     estimates were unrealistic in front of the client [behavior], it
     created an awkward dynamic and made the client question our team's
     alignment [impact]. Here's how I'd suggest handling that in the future."

THINGS TO EMPHASIZE IN YOUR STORY:
  • You gave feedback PROMPTLY (not 6 months later)
  • You gave it PRIVATELY (not in a group setting)
  • You were SPECIFIC and BEHAVIORAL (not personality-based)
  • You gave the person a chance to respond
  • You followed up to see if things changed
  • The relationship remained intact or improved

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SPONSORSHIP vs. MENTORSHIP
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MENTORSHIP: You advise and coach
SPONSORSHIP: You actively advocate for the person in rooms they're not in

Strong senior engineers sponsor their mentees. Examples:
  → "I recommended [name] to lead the Q4 replatforming project."
  → "I advocated for [name] to be promoted at the calibration meeting."
  → "I introduced [name] to my network when she was job searching."
  → "I made sure [name]'s work was visible to the VP in the all-hands."

If you have sponsorship stories, use them — they are rare and impressive.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT MAKES A MENTORING STORY EXCEPTIONAL (vs. AVERAGE)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

AVERAGE STORY:
  "I mentored a junior engineer by answering their questions and reviewing
   their code. They grew a lot and eventually became independent."

EXCEPTIONAL STORY:
  "I diagnosed that their core challenge was confidence under ambiguity, not
   technical skill. I designed a 12-week structured program with weekly
   pairing, deliberate stretch tasks at increasing complexity, and a specific
   'I own this end-to-end' project in week 10. By month 4, they were
   independently designing and delivering features and went on to become
   a mentor to another new hire 6 months later."

The difference: SPECIFICITY, DIAGNOSIS, INTENTIONAL DESIGN, MEASURABLE OUTCOME.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PRACTICE QUESTIONS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. Tell me about the most impactful mentoring relationship you've had.
2. Describe a time you had to give difficult feedback to a colleague or report.
3. Tell me about a time you helped someone on your team who was underperforming.
4. Describe how you develop people around you — give a specific example.
5. Tell me about a time you advocated for someone else's growth or promotion.
6. Describe a time your feedback to someone led to a visible improvement.
