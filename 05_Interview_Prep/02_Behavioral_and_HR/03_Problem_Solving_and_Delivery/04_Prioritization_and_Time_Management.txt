PRIORITIZATION & TIME MANAGEMENT — COMPLETE GUIDE
================================================================================

WHY THIS IS ASSESSED
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
In every company beyond a certain scale, there is always more work to do
than time to do it. Prioritization is not just a personal productivity skill
— it is a team leadership and strategic skill. Interviewers are assessing:

  • Do you understand the difference between urgent and important?
  • Can you communicate trade-offs rather than silently dropping things?
  • Can you align your work with the highest-value organizational goals?
  • Do you protect your team from low-value interruptions?
  • Can you say no constructively?

Amazon LP "Deliver Results": "Leaders focus on the key inputs for their
business and deliver them with the right quality and in a timely fashion."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE PRIORITIZATION PROBLEM
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Most engineers struggle with one of these patterns:

TYPE A: THE FIREFIGHTER
  Always responding to the most urgent request, regardless of strategic value.
  Reactive, high-stress, rarely makes durable progress on important work.

TYPE B: THE PERFECTIONIST
  Focuses exclusively on the "most important" thing and ignores everything else,
  including legitimate urgent needs. Misses deadlines. Frustrates stakeholders.

TYPE C: THE PEOPLE PLEASER
  Says yes to everything, ends up overcommitted, delivers everything late and
  at mediocre quality.

TYPE D: THE PRIORITIZER (what you want to be)
  Classifies work by value and urgency. Communicates trade-offs. Protects the
  high-value work while responsibly managing the rest. Makes commitments they
  can keep.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PRIORITIZATION FRAMEWORKS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE EISENHOWER MATRIX:
  ┌───────────────────┬──────────────────────┐
  │   URGENT + IMPORTANT  │  NOT URGENT + IMPORTANT  │
  │   DO NOW              │  SCHEDULE (big rocks)    │
  ├───────────────────┼──────────────────────┤
  │   URGENT + NOT IMPORTANT  │  NOT URGENT + NOT IMPORTANT  │
  │   DELEGATE or DO FAST     │  ELIMINATE or DEFER INDEFINITELY │
  └───────────────────┴──────────────────────┘

IMPACT × EFFORT MATRIX:
  High impact, low effort  → DO FIRST (quick wins)
  High impact, high effort → PLAN AND PROTECT (strategic bets)
  Low impact, low effort   → BATCH AND DO LATER
  Low impact, high effort  → SAY NO OR DEFER

VALUE + RISK PRIORITIZATION (engineering):
  P0 — Customer-facing outage or data loss. Drop everything.
  P1 — Significant degradation or blocking a major release. Fix today.
  P2 — Important but workaroundable. Fix this week.
  P3 — Nice to have. Fix if time permits.

RICE SCORING (Product + Engineering):
  Score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort
  Higher score = higher priority.

COMMON PRIORITIZATION QUESTION TYPES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. "Tell me about a time you had too much on your plate. How did you handle it?"
2. "Describe a time you had to choose between two competing priorities.
    How did you decide?"
3. "Tell me about a time you had to say no to a request. How did you handle it?"
4. "Describe a time when you had to reprioritize mid-project."
5. "How do you manage your workload when you have multiple competing deadlines?"
6. "Tell me about a time you dropped something important by mistake. What
    happened?"

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FULL EXAMPLE — COMPETING PRIORITIES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Question: "Tell me about a time you had multiple competing priorities and
           not enough time. How did you manage it?"

ANSWER:

[SITUATION]
"In Q3 2023, I was simultaneously responsible for three things:
  1. The final 3 weeks of our mobile app performance rewrite (6-month project,
     imminent customer deadline)
  2. An unexpected P1 production incident — a memory leak in our API gateway
     was causing 5% of requests to fail
  3. Onboarding a new engineer who had just joined the team"

[TASK]
"I was a senior engineer. All three were genuinely important. But I had
 limited hours per day and a team of 2 other engineers."

[ACTION]
"I stepped back and applied a structured triage:

 INCIDENT FIRST (but time-boxed):
   The P1 incident was affecting live users — that was the non-negotiable.
   I spent 3 hours diagnosing the memory leak, identified that a connection
   pool wasn't being released after timeouts, pushed a hotfix, and handed
   monitoring to our on-call rotation. Incident resolved in 4 hours, then
   I stepped away.

 PROTECT THE DELIVERY:
   The performance rewrite had a customer commitment. I explicitly blocked
   2-hour focus blocks in my calendar each morning for the three critical
   remaining tasks: performance benchmarking, regression testing, and a
   final stakeholder demo.

 DELEGATE THE ONBOARDING:
   I redesigned my new hire's first week. Instead of pairing extensively
   myself, I assigned them two well-documented starter tasks and matched
   them with a mid-level teammate for daily pairing. I stayed available for
   30 minutes at the end of each day.

 COMMUNICATE:
   I sent a quick note to my manager that week: 'FYI — carrying three
   priorities simultaneously this week. My plan: [above]. If anything is
   out of order, please flag.' She responded: 'Looks right, let me know
   if you need help prioritizing.'"

[RESULT]
"The performance rewrite shipped on schedule. The P1 was resolved within
 4 hours. The new engineer delivered their first PR by end of week 1.

 My manager specifically called out my prioritization judgment in my
 end-of-quarter performance review as a model for the team."

[REFLECTION]
"The key lesson was: when overloaded, don't try to context-switch frantically.
 Instead, invest 30 minutes at the start to triage and assign dedicated
 time blocks. The 30-minute planning investment pays back many hours."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SAYING NO — THE HARDEST PRIORITIZATION SKILL
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Saying no to a request is one of the hardest professional skills to develop.
Most people either say yes to everything (and underdeliver) or say a blunt
"no" that damages relationships.

THE CONSTRUCTIVE NO:
  1. Acknowledge the request genuinely ("I understand why this is important")
  2. Explain the trade-off ("If I take this on, here's what will be affected")
  3. Offer alternatives ("Could we defer X? Could someone else take Y?")
  4. Or propose timing ("I can't pick this up until [date] — would that work?")

EXAMPLE:
  "I'd love to help with the dashboard redesign. Right now I'm in the final
   two weeks of the payments migration and taking on new work would put that
   at risk. Could this wait until [date], or could [colleague] take the
   initial scoping? I can do a review pass at the end of next week either way."

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TIME MANAGEMENT TOOLS (mention naturally in answers)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  • CALENDAR BLOCKING: "I blocked dedicated 2-hour focus windows for deep work"
  • DAILY PLANNING: "I spend 10 minutes each morning reviewing my priorities"
  • WEEKLY REVIEWS: "On Friday afternoons I review the week and plan the next"
  • EXPLICIT COMMITMENTS: "I track commitments in writing so nothing slips"
  • THE TWO-MINUTE RULE: "If something takes < 2 minutes, I do it immediately"
  • COMMUNICATION BATCHING: "I batch email/Slack review to 3x per day"
