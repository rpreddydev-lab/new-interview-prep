================================================================================
SALARY NEGOTIATION TACTICS: HOW TO MAXIMIZE YOUR OFFER
================================================================================
Purpose: Negotiation is the single highest-ROI activity in a job search.
         A 30-minute negotiation conversation can add $20,000-$100,000+ per year
         to your TC and compounds over your entire career. This guide gives
         you specific scripts, frameworks, and principles to negotiate effectively.
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE NEGOTIATION MINDSET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

TRUTH #1: VIRTUALLY EVERY OFFER IS NEGOTIABLE
  Companies expect candidates to negotiate. Making an offer does not mean they
  are maxed out. Recruiters have ranges; most initial offers are below the top
  of the range. Negotiating is normal and expected.

TRUTH #2: YOU WILL NOT LOSE AN OFFER BY NEGOTIATING (IF YOU DO IT PROFESSIONALLY)
  A company that rescinds an offer because you professionally asked for more
  is a company you don't want to work for. This almost never happens.
  The risk of not negotiating >> the risk of negotiating.

TRUTH #3: THE RECRUITER IS NOT YOUR ENEMY
  Recruiters want to close the deal. Their job is easier if you accept.
  They will advocate for you with the hiring manager if they think they might
  lose you to another offer or if your case is strong.

TRUTH #4: LEVERAGE = ALTERNATIVES
  Your negotiation power is directly proportional to your alternatives.
  One offer: low leverage. Two competing offers: significant leverage.
  Three+ offers: very strong leverage.
  If you have no alternatives, your best leverage is your perceived value
  and the cost to the company of reopening the search.

TRUTH #5: NEVER GIVE A NUMBER FIRST
  Whoever names a number first anchors the negotiation.
  If they ask your expectation and you say $180K, you may have just told them
  their budget was $220K. Keep them named first.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE NEGOTIATION SEQUENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PHASE 1: RECEIVE THE OFFER (DON'T RESPOND IMMEDIATELY)

  When the recruiter calls with the offer:
  - Thank them enthusiastically: "That's exciting news, thank you so much for
    letting me know. I really enjoyed the interview process."
  - Buy time: "I'd love to review the full details. When do you need a decision by?"
  - Get the offer in writing: "Can you send the formal offer letter? I want to
    review all the details carefully before we talk."
  - NEVER accept on the phone in the same call you receive the offer.
    Even if it sounds great. Even if you're thrilled.
    Reason: You can't un-accept. You lose all leverage the moment you say yes.

PHASE 2: RESEARCH (24-48 HOURS)
  - Check Levels.fyi for your exact level and location at this company
  - Check Glassdoor, Blind, LinkedIn for comp data
  - Talk to anyone you know at the company
  - Determine: Is this at the bottom, middle, or top of range for this level?
  - Calculate your target number (top of range + 10-15%)

PHASE 3: COUNTEROFFER (48-72 HOURS AFTER RECEIVING OFFER)
  The goal: get the recruiter to advocate for you with the hiring manager.

  TEMPLATE SCRIPT (PHONE/VIDEO — PREFERRED OVER EMAIL FOR NEGOTIATION):
    "Hi [Recruiter], thanks again for the offer. I'm genuinely excited about
    the role and team — I was very impressed during the interviews.
    I've had a chance to review the details carefully.
    [Optional: "I have a competing offer from [Company] at $X in TC."]
    Based on my research into market rates for this level, and given
    [your specific value: X years in the domain, led a project at scale, etc.],
    I was hoping we could get the base salary to $[your target number].
    Is there flexibility there?"

  THEN: STOP TALKING. Wait for their response. The silence is uncomfortable;
  let them fill it.

PHASE 4: WORK THE LEVERS
  If they can't move on base, ask about other levers:
    "I understand there may not be flexibility on base. Is there any room on
    the signing bonus to help bridge the gap? Even a one-time payment would
    help as I transition."

  "Could we revisit the equity grant? An additional 10-15% in RSUs would
  make a real difference and better aligns me with the company's success."

  "What about the start date? I have an ongoing project I'd like to finish
  responsibly, so a start date 4 weeks from today would work better."

PHASE 5: CONVERGE AND CLOSE
  Once you've pushed to the limit: accept gracefully or decline respectfully.
  Don't drag it out; don't go back more than 2 times on the same lever.
  Set a clear deadline: "If we can get to $X, I'm ready to sign today."

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. SPECIFIC NEGOTIATION SCRIPTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHEN ASKED "WHAT ARE YOUR SALARY EXPECTATIONS?":
  Never give a number. Deflect professionally:
  Option A (if early in process): "I'd prefer to learn more about the full
  scope and compensation package before committing to a number. I'm sure
  we can find something that works for both of us."

  Option B (if late in process and they press): "Based on my research and
  what I'm seeing at similar companies for this level, I'd expect something
  in the range of [$X - $Y]. But I'm happy to consider the full package."
  Use $X as slightly above your real minimum; $Y as your goal.

WHEN THEY SAY "THIS IS THE BEST WE CAN DO":
  "I appreciate that, and I understand there may be constraints. Could you
  help me understand: is it the base salary specifically that's fixed, or
  is there any flexibility in other parts of the package? I'm really excited
  about this opportunity and want to make it work."

WHEN THEY SAY "WE DON'T NEGOTIATE":
  Very few companies truly don't negotiate. But if it's a firm policy:
  "I appreciate you being direct. I am still very interested in this role.
  Could you help me understand if there's flexibility in the signing bonus
  even if base is fixed? I'd like to find a way to make this work."

WHEN YOU HAVE A COMPETING OFFER:
  "I want to be transparent — I have another offer at $[TC]. I genuinely
  prefer this role for [specific reason], but I have to weigh the financial
  impact. If you could get closer to $[target], this is an easy decision."

  IMPORTANT: Only cite a real competing offer. Lying is unethical and blows up badly.

WHEN NEGOTIATING BASE vs. EQUITY:
  If base is hard to move: "Is it possible to increase the equity grant?
  I'm willing to accept the current base if we can increase RSUs by 20-25%."
  
  If equity is uncertain (startup): "Given the early stage and equity risk,
  I'd feel better with a higher base as the guaranteed component."

AFTER THEY IMPROVE THE OFFER:
  "Thank you — I really appreciate you going to bat for me. That improvement
  makes this much more compelling. Let me just verify one thing: if we can
  confirm [start date / one remaining item], I'm ready to sign today."
  → This technique closes the loop while still extracting one small additional win.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHAT CAN BE NEGOTIATED?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALMOST EVERYTHING IN AN OFFER LETTER IS NEGOTIABLE:

  Base Salary:        Most commonly negotiated; often has 10-20% room
  Signing Bonus:      Often easier to move than base (one-time cost to company)
  Annual Bonus Target:Usually harder; tied to level band — ask anyway
  RSU Grant Size:     Yes; especially if stock performance is flat or declining
  RSU Vesting Schedule: Sometimes adjustable; 1-year cliff is standard but not fixed
  Start Date:         Yes, ask for the date that works for you
  Title/Level:        If you feel you're being hired below your actual level, ask
  Remote Work:        Yes, especially now — negotiate % remote days explicitly
  PTO Policy:         If company has standard fixed PTO, ask for extra vacation
  Professional Development: Ask for a training/conference budget
  Home Office Stipend: Increasingly standard; ask if not included
  401k Matching Vesting: Sometimes you can ask this to vest faster
  Relocation Assistance: If relocating, always ask for help

HARD TO NEGOTIATE (but not impossible):
  Benefits plans (usually standardized)
  Stock option exercise price (determined by 409A)
  Bonus formula or metrics

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. ANCHORING: THE MOST POWERFUL NEGOTIATION TECHNIQUE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ANCHORING: The first number named becomes the reference point for the entire
negotiation. Ask high; it shifts the negotiation in your favor.

HOW TO ANCHOR:
  - If they name first (usually they do): your target should be 10-20% above offer
  - If you must name first: add 15-20% above your real target; this is room to come down

EXAMPLE:
  Offer:          $180,000 base
  Your target:    $195,000
  Your anchor:    $205,000
  
  You: "Based on my research, I was expecting closer to $205K."
  Them: "Our max is $192K."
  You: "Could we get to $195K and I'll sign today?"
  Result: $195,000 — your actual target.

Without anchoring at $205K, they may have countered at $185K and landed at $188K.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. BATNA: YOUR BEST ALTERNATIVE TO A NEGOTIATED AGREEMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Your BATNA is what you do if this negotiation fails.
  Strong BATNA (competing offer, current job): negotiate aggressively
  Weak BATNA (unemployed, no other offers): negotiate carefully with more appreciation for getting the role

IMPROVE YOUR BATNA BEFORE NEGOTIATING:
  - Interview at multiple companies simultaneously
  - Time your interviews so offers arrive around the same time
  - If you get one offer early, use it to accelerate other processes:
    "I have an offer I'm considering. Could you tell me where you are in your process?
    I want to make sure I have a complete picture before deciding."

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. NEGOTIATION TIMING AND LOGISTICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHEN TO NEGOTIATE:
  - After receiving a written offer
  - Before signing or verbally accepting
  - If offer was verbal, get written first, then negotiate

HOW TO NEGOTIATE (CHANNEL):
  - Phone/video call: STRONGLY PREFERRED
    - Builds rapport; you can read tone; recruiter is more likely to help in real time
  - Email: acceptable if phone isn't possible; gives you time to craft response
    - Risk: cold, no back-and-forth, easier to say "no" in writing

DEADLINES AND PRESSURE:
  - Exploding offers ("you have 48 hours") are pressure tactics; push back professionally:
    "I'm very interested but I want to make a thoughtful decision. Could we extend
    to [one week from today]? I'd hate to rush and not give this my full attention."
  - Most companies will extend. A company that won't extend a deadline for a senior
    hire is signaling something about their culture.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. COMMON NEGOTIATION MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISTAKE 1: ACCEPTING ON THE SPOT
  You lose 100% of your leverage. NEVER accept immediately. Always take 24-48 hours.

MISTAKE 2: BEING APOLOGETIC
  "I'm sorry to ask, but..." → removes your leverage
  Replace with: "I was hoping we could discuss..." (confident, not apologetic)

MISTAKE 3: LYING ABOUT COMPETING OFFERS
  If they ask for proof and you can't provide it, you've destroyed trust and the offer.

MISTAKE 4: NEGOTIATING OVER EMAIL TOO EARLY
  Email is fine for small asks but for major negotiations, call first.
  "I'd love to discuss a few details — do you have 10 minutes this week?"

MISTAKE 5: GOING BACK TOO MANY TIMES
  2 rounds of negotiation is normal. 3+ can frustrate the recruiter.
  Pick your most important asks and make them count.

MISTAKE 6: FORGETTING THE RECRUITER IS AN ALLY
  They want this to close. They'll advocate for you if you give them a reason.
  "I really want to make this work. Is there anything you can do?"

MISTAKE 7: ACCEPTING BEFORE COMPARING ALL OFFERS
  Even if the first offer is great, wait until you have the full picture
  (or have used the offer to accelerate other processes).
